Abstract
An agent is a model plus a harness: the runtime that couples an LLM to the world through a
loop, tools, context management, safety controls, orchestration, and extension surfaces.Harness
engineering, named as a discipline in early 2026, is the design and evolution of that runtime.
This paper gives the young discipline its most comprehensive empirical foundation to date. It
is a source-code anatomy of eleven production coding harnesses:Claude Code(Anthropic),
Codex CLI(OpenAI),Gemini CLI(Google),Mistral Vibe(Mistral),OpenHands,Aider,
Mini-SWE-Agent,Hermes(Nous Research),Pi,OpenCode, andOpenClaw, plusOm-
nigent(Databricks), the first meta-harness we are aware of, analyzed as a contrast point. The
paper defines what a harness is, maps its seven canonical subsystems with the minimal and max-
imal implementation of each, and dissects all eleven systems along those subsystems. It does not
benchmark or rank; it describes and compares how the systems are built.
The audit yields 13 cross-cutting observations and a catalog of 29 recurring design pat-
terns. Two absences survive a threefold corpus expansion: across roughly four million lines of
Python, TypeScript, and Rust, no agent runtime imports a general-purpose agentic framework
(LangChain, LangGraph, AutoGen, or a dozen others; Gemini CLI uses neither of Google’s
own), and none retrieves code with vector embeddings; the field runs on hand-rolled async loops
and deterministic retrieval (ripgrep, tree-sitter, glob, auto-discovered Markdown context files).
The extensibility standards resolved: SKILL.md skills lead MCP in adoption (9/11 vs. 8/11),
with registries, trust tiers, and the corpus’s first agent-authored skills; ACP ships in six systems
and acquired a third role,harness hosting, with OpenHands running Claude Code, Codex, or
Gemini CLI as interchangeable backends.
Because the original eight systems were re-pinned rather than replaced, the study also con-
tains a controlled longitudinal sample: the same harnesses, source-diffed across one quarter.
The diff shows convergence becomingimitation(Codex adopts Claude Code’s hook vocabulary
verbatim and ships an importer for its sessions and settings; OpenHands reads Claude Code’s
plugin format), while behavioral policy migrates from prompt prose to configuration, and three
of the April edition’s observations required substantive revision in place.
These threads converge on the paper’s thesis: in the first half of 2026 the coding harness
completed a turn from tool toplatform. Harnesses became importable SDKs while framework
vendorsshippedharnesses; marketplaces, switching-costtooling, andenterprisegovernancelayers
appeared; the agent became addressable as a model behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint; and
a meta-harness now orchestrates eleven vendor harnesses (half this corpus among them) behind
one API, re-implementing the expensive parts and arbitraging the proprietary ones. The paper
closes with 18 design recommendations pinned to the observed code, and a 90-line minimum-
viable-harness scaffold that implements ten of them.

1 Introduction
An agent is a model plus a harness. The model supplies the intelligence; the harness turns that
intelligence into work through a loop, tools, context management, safety controls, orchestration, and
extension surfaces. In the span of five months,harness engineeringwent from a phrase coined in
a vendor blog post to a discipline with practitioner guides, formal definitions, automated-evolution
systems, and its own arXiv genealogy (Section 2.1). What the discipline does not yet have is a
reference: an account, grounded in production source code, of what a harness actually is, what it
is made of, and how the field’s leading implementations differ. This paper is that reference, and it
arrives with a thesis: the harness has stopped being a tool and become aplatform.
The existing literature reaches neither goal. Benchmark surveys report scores (typically SWE-
Bench [1]) without touching architecture; conceptual taxonomies [5, 6] describe patterns without
implementations; the one concurrent source-level taxonomy [34] excludes the provider-native sys-
tems that define the field’s frontier. None reaches the engineering decisions that determine whether
a harness is reliable, extensible, or deployable: how the loop is structured, how tools are defined
and sandboxed, how safety is enforced, how context is rationed. Nor does any of it ask where the
category itself is heading.
We study eleven systems at source level, pinned to July 2026 releases. Four are the flagship
products of the major commercial LLM providers:Claude Code(Anthropic, TypeScript, via a
circulated source snapshot),Codex CLI(OpenAI, Rust),Gemini CLI(Google, TypeScript),
andMistral Vibe(Mistral AI, Python). Seven are open source, spanning the field’s full spread:
OpenHands(event-sourced SDK, since mid-2026 also a host for rival harnesses),Aider(thirteen
polymorphic edit formats),Mini-SWE-Agent(the 100-line research floor),Hermes(the fastest-
growing harness on GitHub, with a self-improving skill loop),Pi(the minimal-core countertrend),
OpenCode(the most-starred dedicated coding agent, with the corpus’s most thoroughgoing clien-
t/server architecture), andOpenClaw(a multi-channel gateway, our non-SWE contrast point). A
twelfth system,Omnigent(Databricks), is the firstmeta-harness, an orchestration layer over the
others, and is analyzed as a second contrast point. Because eight of these systems were already
audited in this study’s April 2026 edition, their retained snapshots yield a controlled longitudinal
sample: the same harnesses, source-diffed across one quarter.
The paper makes seven contributions. The first is the discipline’s reference anatomy (Section 2):
a definition of the harness with its genealogy and boundary cases, and a component map of the
seven canonical subsystems, each with its minimal and maximal observed implementation, that
organizes the rest of the paper. On that map the paper builds a systematic source-level comparison
of eleven harnesses, the first corpus to cover all four provider-native systems, the two most-adopted
open-source harnesses, and each system’ssignaturecapability (Table 4). The audit yields 13 cross-
cutting observations and a catalog of 29 recurring patterns, several documented here for the first
time: agent-maintained memory pipelines, verify-on-stop guards, lineage compaction, session-tree
version control, log-as-queue loops, syntax-aware command permissioning, cache-dialect fanout, and
harness mimicry.
Because the original eight systems were re-pinned rather than replaced, the study also carries a
longitudinal analysis (Section 14.5): ninety days of harness evolution measured in source, in which
convergence becomes imitation (hook vocabularies copied verbatim, plugin formats adopted, session
importers shipped), patterns diffuse down the corpus, and behavioral policy migrates from prompt
prose to configuration. Two empirical absences are established at eleven-system scale and survive a
threefold corpus expansion: no agent runtime uses a general-purpose agentic framework, and none
uses embedding-based retrieval over code; their historical resolution lies in the harness–framework
merger. That merger opens onto the platform-turn thesis (Section 14), documented in named
artifacts: harness SDKs and framework-built harnesses converging on one shape, plugin and skill

2 What Is a Harness?
Before comparing implementations, we fix the object of study. This section defines the harness,
traces the short history of the term, separates it from the concepts it is routinely confused with,
and lays out the seven subsystems that every harness in our corpus implements in some form. A
reader new to the field should leave this section knowing exactly what a harness is and what it is
made of; the rest of the paper substantiates each cell of the map against production source code.
2.1 Definition and Genealogy
An agent is a model plus a harness. The harness is everything except the model: the
runtime that couples an LLM to the world—its loop, its tools, its context, its safety con-
trols, its orchestration, and its extension surfaces. Harness engineering is the discipline
of designing and evolving that runtime.
The one-line algebraAgent = Model + Harnesswas popularized by Trivedy’s LangChain
engineering series in early 2026 [28], and the termharness engineeringentered circulation in Febru-
ary 2026: first used in passing by Mitchell Hashimoto, then defined and elaborated by Trivedy in the
context of LangChain’s Deep Agents work [29].1 The discipline acquired its practitioner literature
within weeks: Böckeler’s guide to harness engineeringfor coding-agent users[30] (April), O’Reilly
and independent syntheses (June), and community-curated pattern catalogs. An academic wave
followed the same arc: Lin et al.’sAgentic Harness Engineering[85] automates harness evolution;
HARBOR [35] optimizes harnesses against benchmarks; Wang et al. recast code itself as the harness
substrate [36]; Rombaut’s source-code taxonomy of thirteen open-source scaffolds [34] is the closest
methodological relative of the present study (Section 3.3 maps his twelve dimensions onto our seven
subsystems); and Macedo [33] gives the concept its first operational definition, deriving necessary
and sufficient conditions for what counts as a harness. We adopt Macedo’s definitional core—the
layer that wraps a language model and turns it into an agent able to act on a repository—and extend
it with the subsystem decomposition of Section 2.3, which the definitional literature gestures at but
has not yet grounded in production source.

Usage of the term is loose, and four boundary cases account for most of the confusion [33].

3 Background and Related Work
3.1 LLM-Powered Code Agents
The concept of using LLMs as autonomous coding agents gained traction with SWE-Bench [1], a
benchmark of real-world GitHub issues requiring end-to-end resolution. SWE-Agent [2] introduced
theAgent-Computer Interface(ACI), providing LLMs with specialized file viewing and editing tools.
The CodeAct paradigm [3] proposed unifying agent actions into executable code, arguing that this
subsumes discrete tool calling. ReAct [4] established the reasoning-action loop that underpins most
current agent architectures.
A second wave of SWE-specific agents has appeared since. AutoCodeRover [44] replaces text-
based search with AST-aware retrieval and spectrum-based fault localization. MASAI [48] de-
composes the issue-resolution pipeline into modular sub-agents and reports cost-aware results on
SWE-Bench Lite. MAGIS [47] and CodeR [46] both formalize role-based multi-agent coordina-
tion for issue resolution (Manager/Custodian/Developer/QA in MAGIS; explicit task graphs in
CodeR). Lingma SWE-GPT [49] trains a base model jointly with the SE process loop, an instance
of the model–agent co-design we examine in Section 7. SWE-Search [52] layers Monte Carlo tree
search over agent trajectories (continuing the line of search-based reasoning developed in Tree of
Thoughts [70] and LATS [73], with Plan-and-Solve [71] as the simpler precursor), and Diversity-
Empowers-Intelligence [53] ensembles heterogeneous agents under a meta-policy. At the other end of
the design spectrum, Agentless [51] reports that a fixed localize/repair/validate pipeline is compet-
itive with agent loops on SWE-Bench Lite, a useful counterweight when discussing whether agentic
complexity is necessary. The OpenHands platform paper [45] provides peer-reviewed grounding
for the system whose source we audit in Section 6. Concurrent with this study, Lin et al. [85]
proposeAgentic Harness Engineering(AHE), which automatically evolves agent harnesses (tools,
middleware, memory, sub-agent configuration) using observability-driven feedback. Starting from
a bash-only seed comparable toMini-SWE-Agent, their system reaches 71.9% on SWE-Bench
Verified, and the evolved harness transfers across model families. Their component-level ablation
provides quantitative evidence for the architectural emphases we arrive at independently through
source-code analysis: tools, middleware, and long-term memory carry the improvement, while the
system prompt alone does not.
3.2 Agent Architecture Surveys
Prior work on agent architectures [5, 6, 66] has identified common patterns including tool use, mem-
ory management, and planning. The multi-agent dimension is covered in detail by Guo et al. [67];
the SWE-specific subset is mapped by Liu et al. [68]; AgentBench [69] provides the standard cross-
domainevaluationharness. Thesesurveysoperateataconceptuallevel, describingwhatcomponents
exist rather than how they are implemented. The present work complements them by grounding
architectural analysis in concrete implementations, showing that the “same” pattern (e.g., tool use)
admits considerable variation in practice.
3.3 Concurrent Source-Code Taxonomies
The closest methodological relative of this study is Rombaut’sInside the Scaffold[34], developed
concurrently with our April edition: a source-code taxonomy of thirteen open-source coding-agent
scaffolds at pinned commits, organized as twelve dimensions in three layers (control architecture;
tool/environment interface; resource management), with the useful finding that most agentscom-
poseloop primitives (ReAct, generate-test-repair, plan-execute, retry, tree search) rather than im-
plementing a single one. The two studies are complementary rather than redundant. Rombaut’s
corpus is open-source-only, skews toward benchmark-oriented pipelines (AutoCodeRover, Agentless,

3.4 Multi-Agent Systems for Software Engineering
Recent work has explored multi-agent collaboration for software engineering. MetaGPT [7] assigns
specialized roles (product manager, architect, engineer) to different agents. ChatDev [50], developed
independently, organizes a role-playing development pipeline around chat-chain communication.
AutoGen [8] provides a framework for multi-agent conversations. Magentic-One [9] implements an
orchestratorpatternwithspecializedsub-agents. Forcodingspecifically, MAGIS[47]andCodeR[46]
pair role decomposition with task graphs, and Chain-of-Agents [72] sequences sub-agents over long
inputs. Several systems in the present study incorporate multi-agent patterns, enabling direct
comparison of these approaches within a single domain.
3.5 Tool Use in Language Models
Toolformer [10] demonstrated that LLMs can learn to use external tools. Gorilla [11] showed that
LLMs can generate accurate API calls when provided with documentation. ToolLLM [58] scaled
tool-use evaluation to 16,000+ real-world APIs, and AnyTool [59] demonstrated hierarchical tool
routing for large API surfaces. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) [12] proposes a standard
interface for connecting LLMs to external tools and data sources. The analysis here finds that MCP
has emerged as a cross-system extensibility standard, adopted by eight of the eleven systems studied
(all four provider-native agents plusOpenHands,Hermes,OpenCode, andOpenClaw).
3.6 Vendor-Authored Engineering Guidance
A complementary thread to the academic surveys is engineering guidance published directly by ma-
jor LLM vendors. Between December 2024 and September 2025, Anthropic released a series of four
articles that collectively articulate a coherent design philosophy for agents: (i)Building Effective
Agents[16], which contrasts “workflows” (predefined code paths) with “agents” (LLM-directed dy-
namic processes), enumerates five composable patterns (prompt chaining, routing, parallelization,
orchestrator-workers, evaluator-optimizer), and warns explicitly against general-purpose agentic
frameworks; (ii)Effective Context Engineering for AI Agents[19], which reframes “prompt en-
gineering” as “context engineering,” documentscontext rotin long windows, and recommends a
hybrid approach that favors just-in-time (JIT) retrieval viagrep/tail/file-system while acknowl-
edging that pre-indexed retrieval can complement JIT methods for specific use cases; (iii)Writing
Effective Tools for AI Agents[18], which builds on the agent-computer-interface (ACI) concept in-
troduced inBuilding Effective Agents[16], recommends consolidating tools into “few thoughtful”
high-signal operations, and reports SWE-Bench gains from prompt-engineered tool descriptions;
(iv)How We Built Our Multi-Agent Research System[17], a case study reporting that orchestrator-
worker architectures with parallel sub-agents outperformed single-agent baselines by 90.2% on in-
ternal evaluation while consuming∼15×more tokens than a single chat interaction (the comparison
is against a simple chat baseline, not against an optimized single-agent pipeline).

3.7 The 2026 Harness Landscape
The corpus studied in this paper is a deliberate sample from a field that has grown considerably
faster than the literature tracking it. A market sweep conducted for this revision (July 2026)
identified over two dozen actively maintained coding-agent harnesses beyond the systems analyzed
here; Table 2 lists the most significant. Three developments since the first version of this study
(April 2026) reshape the context in which the corpus sits.3
Every vendor now ships a harness, and the market is consolidating.Beyond the four
provider-native systems we analyze in depth, GitHub’s Copilot CLI reached general availability
in February 2026, Amazon folded its Q Developer CLI into the spec-drivenKirobrand, and xAI
shippedGrok Build[26] in May 2026—notably, a closed-source binary (Rust, per its release ar-
tifacts) whose documentation describes wholesale adoption of the open ecosystem’s conventions:
AGENTS.mdcontext files, MCP servers, skills, hooks, and an ACP server mode for editor embedding.
Consolidation followed: SpaceX acquired xAI in February 2026 and announced a $60B acquisition
of Cursor (Anysphere) in June; Google announced in May that Gemini CLI would transition toward
anAntigravity CLI, then ended Gemini CLI service for its consumer tiers on June 18 (enterprise
Gemini Code Assist licenses retain access and updates). The Google transition is a licensing turn
as much as a rebrand: Gemini CLI is Apache-2.0, developed in the open to∼105k stars, while Anti-
gravity CLI ships as a closed-source Go binary with no published source—so of the three corpus
systems whose vendors develop them in the open (Codex,Gemini CLI,Mistral Vibe), one now
has a closed successor, and our v0.50.0 pin captures the last major open-source state of a provider-
native harness lineage. The harness layer, in other words, is no longer a greenfield: it is strategic
infrastructure that vendors buy, rebrand, and gate.
The open-source field has its own centers of gravity.OpenCode[24] is now the most-
starred dedicated coding agent on GitHub (∼184k stars), andHermes[22] the fastest-growing
by star-accrual rate (∼212k stars within five months of release; counts as of 2026-07-10, as in
Table 2); both enter our corpus in this revision, together withPi[23], the poster child of the oppo-
site, minimal-core countertrend. Block donated its Rust harnessGooseto the Linux Foundation’s
Agentic AI Foundation in April 2026, the first vendor-neutral governance story we identified in
the space. A community project,Claw Code[27], reimplemented Claude Code’s architecture in
Python and Rust as a self-described clean-room derivative of the same March 2026 source exposure
ourClaude Codeanalysis draws on, reaching∼100k stars within days—suggesting that harness
architectureitself, not just model access, commands community interest in its own right. A parallel
wave of Chinese-vendor harnesses (Alibaba’s Qwen Code, a Gemini CLI fork; Moonshot’s Kimi CLI;
ByteDance’s Trae Agent) ties open-weight model releases to their own CLIs. MeanwhileAider, one
of the field’s pioneering systems, entered de-facto community-maintenance mode (last stable release
February 2026), with architectural development continuing in a community fork (aider-ce).
A meta-layer has appeared.Databricks open-sourcedOmnigent[25] in June 2026: an or-
chestration layer that treats entire harnesses (Claude Code,Codex,OpenCode,Hermes,Pi,
Cursor) as interchangeable components behind a common API, adding cross-harness policies, a
uniform OS sandbox, and shareable multi-device sessions. Section 14.4 analyzes it as this paper’s
second contrast point; its existence is itself a datum for the platformization argument of Section 14.1.

6 Agent Loop Design
The agent loop, the central control flow that alternates between LLM inference and action execution,
is the architectural backbone of every SWE agent. We identify three distinct paradigms across the
studied systems.

6.1 Taxonomy of Agent Loop Paradigms
All eleven systems implement variations of the ReAct [4] pattern, but the implementations differ
radically in complexity, concurrency, and state management. We identify three paradigms:iterative
action-observation(nine systems),reflection-augmented(Aider, withHermesrelocating the same
function into the loop’s stop condition), andcoordinator-worker(an overlay on the iterative loop,
prescriptive inClaude CodeandCodex, configuration-gated inHermes). Rombaut’s concurrent
taxonomy [34] makes the useful finer-grained point that most production loopscomposeprimitives
(ReAct, generate-test-repair, plan-execute, retry) rather than implementing one; the plan modes
that all four provider-native systems now ship (Section 10) are plan-execute grafted onto ReAct.
The field’s vocabulary is still being minted around this subsystem.Loop engineeringentered
circulation in June 2026, between this study’s two snapshots: Steinberger (OpenClaw’s original
author) condensed the shift into a widely circulated dictum—stop prompting coding agents, “design
loops that prompt your agents” [31]—and Osmani named and structured the practice days later,
giving the loop an anatomy of triggers, topology, verifiers, and stop rules [32]. The coinage is
complementary to this section’s subject rather than a synonym for it: harness engineering builds
theinneraction–observation cycle dissected below, while loop engineering composes that cycle,
from the outside, into self-sustaining outer loops that prompt, verify, and re-run the agent without
a human authoring each turn. The seam between the two disciplines is already visible in the corpus
as theouter verification looppattern (Table 12):OpenHands’s/goalendpoint runs an LLM judge
over each finished run and either re-prompts or stops, andHermes’s verify-on-stop guard vetoes
the inner loop’s own exit—harness features whose only purpose is to close an outer loop around the
inner one.
6.2 Iterative Action-Observation Loop
The most common pattern follows a cycle of prompt construction, LLM inference, tool execution,
and observation collection. Nine systems implement this pattern, each with distinctive characteris-
tics.
OpenHands: Event-Sourced Conversation Engine.OpenHands’s loop—since the mid-
2026 restructuring, in thesoftware-agent-sdkrepository rather than the application repo—is
an event-sourced conversation engine: aLocalConversationdrivesAgent.step(), every event
is appended to a persistentEventLog(one file per event through aFileStore, with flock-based
locking and secret redaction via aSecretRegistry), and the LLM’s view of history is a cached
projection of the active branch. Conversation state is atreewith a movable head, so replay, fork,
and branch navigation are first-class. Eachstep()makes one LLM call but executesalltool calls of
the response as an action batch—optionally in parallel through aParallelToolExecutorgoverned
by atool_concurrency_limitand a resource-lock manager keyed on each tool’s declared resources
(files, terminal session, browser), so only same-resource calls serialize (theClaude Codeparagraph
below and Table 18 contrast this withClaude Code’s boolean partitioning). TheStuckDetector
survives the rearchitecture intact: the same five failure scenarios as in the V0 codebase (repeating
action-observation pairs, repeating actions with errors, monologue loops, alternating patterns, and
context-window error loops), now with configurable thresholds and enabled by default.
Claude Code: Streaming Loop with Concurrent Tool Batching.Claude Code’s loop re-
sponsesstreamviaServer-SentEvents(SSE)withfine-grainedeventhandling(content_block_start,
content_block_delta,content_block_stop). The most distinctive feature isintelligent tool
batching: tool calls are partitioned into batches by concurrency safety via a single-pass reduce
algorithm. Tools default toisConcurrencySafe = false(the safe default), and must explicitly
opt in to parallel execution. Read-only tools (grep, glob, file read) are safe; write tools (edit,
bash) are not. This design enables significant latency reduction when the LLM requests multiple
independent reads simultaneously.

6.3 Reflection-Augmented Loop
Aiderextends the basic loop with areflectionmechanism. Therun_one()method implements a
nested loop: after each LLM response, the system applies edits, then checks for lint errors (via a
three-stage Python pipeline: syntax check→compile check→flake8), test failures, and unresolved
file mentions. If issues are detected, areflected_messagetriggers re-invocation of the LLM with
corrective context, up to a configurable maximum (default: 3 reflections).
The linter integration is particularly notable: it usesTreeContextfromgrep_astto show code
context around error lines, providing the LLM with precise locality information for its corrections.
Aiderremains the only system whosemain loopis reflection-shaped, but the corpus has grown
three structural cousins:Gemini CLI’s edit tool ends in an LLM “edit fixer” subcall that repairs
a failed match (Section 8.4),OpenCodefeeds LSP diagnostics back into every edit result, and
Hermes’s verify-on-stop guard performs the reflection check once, at turn exit, instead of per
iteration. The pattern is the in-the-wild instantiation of a line of work on language-model self-
correction, Reflexion [54], Self-Refine [55], CRITIC [56], and Self-Debug [57], that has otherwise
stayed largely in the academic literature.Aider’s contribution is the integration: lint and test
signals are routed back into the loop as the corrective feedback those papers theorize about. A note
on its stop condition:Aiderhas no tool-call loop at all—a turn ends when the completion stream
finishes, andrun_oneexits when no reflection is pending or the cap is reached; it never inspects an
end_turnfinish reason.
6.4 Coordinator-Worker Pattern
Claude CodeandCodexsupport acoordinator modewhere a parent agent orchestrates multiple
worker agents;Hermesgates the same shape behind configuration (anorchestratorrole plus
a spawn-depth setting unlocks nested delegation trees, and a separate Kanban-swarm mode runs
planning-root→workers→verifier as subprocesses over a SQLite blackboard). This is an overlay
on the iterative loop, adding a hierarchical dispatch layer.
InClaude Code, the coordinator spawns sub-agents viaAgentTool, each receiving a forked
context with isolatedAbortController, cloned file state cache, and suppressed permission dialogs.
Workers communicate results back via<task-notification>XML blocks, and the coordinator
synthesizes findings before delegating the next phase. The workflow is structured: Research→
Synthesis→Implementation→Verification.
InCodex, sub-agents are spawned viaAgentControl::spawn_agent(), receiving dedicated
ThreadIds and communicating through typedMailboxchannels. Each thread maintains its own his-
tory, withSpawnAgentForkModecontrolling context inheritance (FullHistoryorLastNTurns(N)).
We defer the detailed analysis of multi-agent orchestration to Section 11.

in the system. The corpus spans the full range from tight single-provider coupling to complete
provideragnosticism, andthepositiononthatspectrumhasdirectimplicationsfortheoptimizations
available to the scaffold.
7.1 Provider Abstraction Spectrum
The eleven systems adopt five distinct strategies:
Single-provider tight coupling (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google).Claude Codeexclusively

on Claude-specific features (extended thinking, prompt caching with static/dynamic boundaries,
tool_useblock format);Codexdepends on OpenAI’s Responses API wire format and maintains
model-specific prompts per GPT generation, delivered as of mid-2026 asserver-side model-catalog

7.3 Prompt Content and Rhetorical Style
The previous subsection examinedhowprompts are assembled (cache boundaries, templates, hier-
archical merges). We now turn towhat they actually say: the persona each scaffold projects, the
explicit directives it issues to the model, and the rhetorical devices it uses to make those directives
stick. We read the canonical system prompt of each system and extracted recurring axes. Several
patterns prove convergent across otherwise unrelated codebases.
Identity and persona.Persona openings range from terse to elaborate.Mini-SWE-Agentis

9.3 Recursive Summarization (Aider)

internal sub-agent (no approvals, no network, local-write-only) reviewing a git-style workspace diff.

10 Safety and Permission Models
Safety mechanisms become important in direct proportion to the autonomy the agent is given. A
recent line of academic work has formalized the threat model: indirect prompt injection through
file or web content [77], AgentDojo [76] as a dynamic evaluation environment for injection attacks
and defenses, risk-awareness benchmarks like R-Judge [78], and ToolSword’s [79] catalog of tool-
use safety failure modes. The corpus covers a wide range, from systems with essentially no safety
controls to multi-layered architectures.

10.3 OpenHands: Ensemble Defense-in-Depth
OpenHands’s pluggableSecurityAnalyzerframework matured substantially in the V1 SDK.

10.4 OpenClaw: Scope-Based Authorization

limit provides DoS protection (CWE-400).

every tool call into one of four modes:
•PLAN: Read-only; the model sees the full tool list but can only invoke read tools (Glob,

before exiting plan mode.
•DEFAULT: Interactive approval dialog per tool call; users can approve once, approve always,
or reject.
•AUTO_EDIT: Edit operations are auto-approved while shell and other side-effecting tools
still prompt; intended for fast iteration on local code.
•YOLO: All actions auto-approved. Intended for CI/scripted use only.

10.6 Mistral Vibe: Permission-Scope Patterns Plus Agent-Profile Gates
Mistral Vibehas no OS sandbox (an opt-in–worktreeflag now provides branch-level isolation)

2. Tool-specific validators (e.g.,resolve_file_tool_permissionpattern-matches paths), plus

3. User-declared shell hooks (hooks.toml,before_tool) that can deny a call or rewrite its

10.7 Hermes: A Policy Floor That Survives YOLO
Hermesinverts the sandbox question: the largest Python system in the corpus shipszeroOS-
level isolation primitives—no Seatbelt, Landlock, seccomp, or Bubblewrap anywhere in its tools
tree—delegating isolation to its six pluggable execution backends. On the local backend, safety
ispolicy-as-code at scale: a 3,200-line approval module whose documentation states “config.yaml
IS the security policy.” Its layers: user deny-globs; a twelve-patternhardline floor(rm -rf

ment variable is frozen at module import precisely so a prompt-injected skill cannot flip it at
runtime; 47 dangerous-command patterns matched ondeobfuscatedvariants (quote-splice removal,
command-substitution folding, command-position anchoring); an external Rust content scanner
(Tirith, cosign-verified install); and an optional auxiliary-LLM gate (_smart_approve: temper-

provals). The distinctive threat model iscontent-borne: promptware scanning of context files,
memory writes, MCP tool descriptions, and skill installs (with builtin/trusted/community trust

absences: no per-tool allow/ask/deny matrix, no append-only audit log.

10.9 OpenCode: Syntax-Aware Permissioning, Permissive by Default
OpenCodeis, afterHermes, the second-largest system shipping no OS-level isolation (“sandbox”

with web-tree-sitter (bash and PowerShell grammars); the exact command text becomes the ask pat-

SQLite; headless runs auto-reject all asks. There is no dangerous-command denylist and no risk
classifier.
10.10 Minimal Safety (Aider, Mini-SWE-Agent)
Aideroffers an interactive confirmation mode but no automated risk assessment. However, its
reflection loop with three-stage Python linting (syntax→compile→flake8) provides implicit safety
by catching errors before they propagate.
Mini-SWE-Agentimplements only resource limits as safety mechanisms—step_limit,
cost_limit, and (added in the v2.4 line) a wall-clock limit and a consecutive-format-error cap,
plus an opt-in interactive confirm mode—sufficient for benchmark evaluation but inadequate for
production use.

11.2 Deep Dive: Claude Code Recursive Composition
Claude Code’s multi-agent architecture is the most sophisticated among the systems studied. It
supports three levels of coordination: ad-hoc sub-agents, typed agent definitions, and full coordina-
tor mode.
11.2.1 Agent Definitions System
Agents are defined via a JSON/Zod schema specifying a description, prompt, optional tool
whitelist/blacklist, model selection, turn limits, permission mode, MCP server bindings, isolation
level, background execution flag, and skills.
Agent definitions are loaded from a priority hierarchy: built-in agents→plugin agents→
user settings→project settings→flag settings→policy/managed agents. Later sources override
earlier ones. Built-in agents include Explore (fast codebase search), Plan (architecture design), and
VerificationAgent (test execution).
11.2.2 Context Forking
When a sub-agent is spawned viaAgentTool, the parent’s context is forked along six dimensions:
1.AbortController: New controller linked to parent (parent abort→child abort, but not vice
versa).
2.File State Cache: LRU cache cloned to prevent concurrent mutation.
3.Permission Prompts: Suppressed for async agents (shouldAvoidPermissionPrompts =
true).
4.App State: Set to no-op for async agents to prevent dead session mutation.
5.Denial Tracking: Fresh local state (refusals don’t accumulate globally).

Mistral Vibe’s multi-agent support is intentionally simple: a parent agent invokes thetask
tool, which constructs a freshAgentLoopinstance with the requested agent profile (e.g.,explore),
runs it to completion in-process, and returns the sub-agent’sAssistantEventoutputs and
ToolResultEventsummaries to the parent. A guard enforces that only profiles tagged as “sub-
agent” (currently justexplore) can be delegated to, preventing accidental recursive escalation to
write-capable profiles. Each sub-agent gets a freshVibeConfig, its own session directory, and an
independent message history, providing isolation comparable toCodex’s thread tree but at the
cost of true parallelism: the parent blocks onawaituntil the sub-agent exits. The architectural
simplicity (one tool, sequential, in-process) meansMistral Vibesits on thesimplicity endof the
multi-agent spectrum—the corpus’s simplest delegation short of none, reminiscent ofOpenHands’s
retired V0AgentDelegateAction.
11.6 Gemini CLI: Registry, Symmetric Session Protocol, and A2A
Gemini CLI’s multi-agent model splits across two layers. Locally, anAgentRegistryholds named
agent definitions—Markdown files with mandatory YAML frontmatter—loaded from user, project,
and extension scopes. The built-ingeneralistagent, for example, grants access to all registered
tools with a 10-minute, 20-turn run budget. Invocation flows through the/agentslash command,
@agent-nameprompt notation, or—since the sub-agent unification of v0.39—a model-invocable
invoke_agenttool, each run receiving an isolated tool registry. The invocation path has been
43
=== PAGE 44 ===
abstracted behind anagent session protocolwith symmetric local and remote implementations, so
a registry entry backed by an in-process executor and one backed by a remote A2A agent are driven
through the same interface.
WhereGemini CLIdiverges from every other system in our corpus is thepackages/a2a-server
module, an experimental implementation of Google’sAgent-to-Agent(A2A) protocol. A2A is to
inter-agent communication what MCP is to tool integration: a JSON-RPC standard for one agent
process to discover, authenticate to, and exchange messages with another, possibly on a different
machine and built by a different vendor.Gemini CLI’s A2A server lets a remote orchestrator drive a
localGemini CLIinstance as if it were a registered local agent—now with usage metadata reported
over the protocol for cross-vendor resource accounting—opening the door to cross-vendor multi-
agent topologies that no other corpus system supports natively (the meta-harness of Section 14.4
builds exactly such topologies from outside).
11.7 Hermes: Guarded Delegation, Swarms, and Mixture-of-Agents
Hermesis flat-by-default delegation with two escape hatches upward.delegate_taskbuilds
child agentsin-processon a thread pool: fresh conversation, focused system prompt, summary-
only return, toolsets intersected with the parent’s (“subagent must not gain tools the parent
lacks”), and a six-tool blocklist (no recursion, no user interaction, no memory writes, no schedul-
ing). Defaults are conservative—three concurrent children, 50-iteration budgets, depth 1—but a
role="orchestrator"configuration plus a spawn-depth setting unlocks nested trees, with the or-
chestrator prompt block explicitly “modeled on OpenClaw’sbuildSubagentSystemPrompt.” Back-
ground delegation returns a handle and re-enters as afresh turnthrough a completion queue rather
than splicing mid-turn—a prompt-cache-preserving choice. Above this sits a process-level tier: the
Kanban swarmruns planning-root→parallel workers→verifier→synthesizer as separatehermes
-p <profile>subprocesses coordinated through a SQLite board and a blackboard of structured
JSON comments—coordinator-worker via shared database rather than protocol or channel. A third
overlay,/moa, fans out advisory reference models (up to eight concurrently) whose outputs season
the main model’s next iteration.
11.8 Pi: Sub-Agents as an Extension, Fleets as a Package
CorePihas no spawn or task tool—sub-agents are the flagship demonstration of its extension
thesis, shipped as a∼1,000-line example. Each invocation spawns a separatepi –mode json -p
–no-sessionOS process, giving hard context isolation by construction; the parent parses the
child’s JSONL stdout for progress and cost aggregation. One tool offers three shapes:single,
parallel(worker pool of four, max eight tasks), andchainwith{previous}substitution. Agent
definitions are Markdown-plus-frontmatter with tool filtering via the child’s–toolsflag; there is no
depth counter and no mid-run parent-child messaging. Above the single process, an experimental
orchestratorpackage supervises fleets ofPiinstances in RPC mode and registers presence with
a hosted coordinator—multi-instancemanagement rather than an in-loop coordinator. Notably,
whenPidoes do multi-agent, it lands oncross-process JSONLrather than in-process primitives—a
data point Section 13.3 returns to.
11.9 OpenCode: Child Sessions with Permission-Derived Capabilities
OpenCode’s sub-agents are child sessions, not a separate engine: thetasktool creates a Ses-
sion with aparentIDrunning the same loop, resumable viatask_id, returning only the final
assistant text wrapped in<task_result>XML; background results are injected as synthetic
user messages. Agents and modes are unified in one schema (mode: primary|subagent|all):
built-ins arebuild,plan(edit denied except plan files),general, read-onlyexplore, plus
hiddencompaction/title/summaryutility agents—plan mode is a permission ruleset plus a
<system-reminder>injection, not an architectural overlay. Tool availability is everywhere
permission-derived(denying"*"for a tool removes it from the LLM’s view), and containment

=== PAGE 45 ===
is asymmetric: children inherit only the parent’s deny rules. Recursion is off by default (task
auto-denied to children) but opt-in per agent with no numeric depth cap; custom agents come
from config, Markdown files, or—uniquely—LLM generation(generateObjectover{identifier,
whenToUse, systemPrompt}).
11.10 OpenClaw Session-Based Orchestration
OpenClaw’s multi-agent model is fundamentally different: it operates through an ACP (Agent
Client Protocol, Zed/Google’s editor-oriented JSON-RPC standard at agentclientprotocol.com, not
IBM’s older homonymous Agent Communication Protocol) translator that bridges the Gateway wire
protocol to agent processes. Sub-agents are spawned as child processes with RPC binding. Each
agent gets its own session with JSONL transcript persistence. Communication occurs via ACP delta
events (text, thought, tool calls) rather than shared memory. A rate limiter (MAX_PROMPT_BYTES =
2MB) provides DoS protection.

12 Extensibility Mechanisms
Every system in the corpus provides extension points, though through quite different mechanisms.
12.1 Plugin Architectures
OpenClawprovides the most mature plugin architecture in the corpus—and since the spring 2026
releases, anexternalizedone: official provider and channel plugins were cut over to standalone first-
class npm packages with manifest-first metadata, moving model catalogs and routing tables out of
core. Plugins are manifest-driven (openclaw.plugin.json), discovered in the workspace directory,
loaded dynamically via jiti, and registered through typed SDK contracts:
•Channel plugins: ImplementChannelEntry(receive, send, status, configure).
•Provider plugins: ImplementProviderEntry(inference stream).
•Skill plugins: Register via capability system.
Strict import boundaries are enforced: extension production code may only import the public
plugin-SDK surface and local API barrels. Direct imports ofsrc/**are prohibited.
OpenClawis, however, no longer alone in the manifest-driven-plugin camp.Codex’s plugin
architecture expanded substantially between our snapshots: a marketplace-add pipeline (GitHub,
git, local paths, URLs), a model-callable in-conversation approval tool (request_plugin_install),
.claude-pluginformat compatibility, and workspace-level sharing joined an already-present plugin
manager; plugins contribute tools, skills, and lifecycle hooks.OpenHands’s plugin system makes
the convergence explicit: its plugins bundle skills, hooks, MCP config, agents, and commands, and
its manifest loader accepts the directories[".plugin", ".claude-plugin"]withplugin.json—it
deliberately readsClaude Code’s plugin format, the clearest interoperability-by-adoption case in
the corpus.Claude Codeitself supports MCP servers (for external tools), skills (for specialized
workflows), and agent definitions (for custom agent types) via a JSON/Zod schema with built-in,
custom, and plugin sources (Section 11.2).Pirelocates the entire question: extensions are runtime-
loaded TypeScript modules against an API of∼33 typed events spanning session lifecycle, tool inter-
ception (blocking and in-place argument mutation), whole-context rewriting, and—unusually—raw
provider I/O surgery (before_provider_request,after_provider_response); the repo ships 78
examples that are, feature for feature, other harnesses’ built-ins, most at 1–16KB each, distributed
through a package manager (pi install npm:...|git:...).OpenCode’s plugins are async func-
tions returning∼20 typed hooks (chat.params,tool.execute.before/after,tool.definition
rewriting, multi-step OAuthauthflows), executed sequentially for determinism; custom tools need
no plugin at all—any.opencode/tool/*.tsexport becomes a tool.
Gemini CLI: Extensions + Slash Commands + Early MCP Adoption.Gemini CLI
ships an explicitExtensionLoaderinterface backed by theGeminiCLIExtensiontype. Extensions
can contribute custom tools (typically via MCP), slash commands, hooks, skills, andGEMINI.md
snippets, and are discovered from the~/.gemini/extensions/directory.Gemini CLIwas an early
adopter of MCP, with first-class implementations forstdio-spawned local servers, SSE connections,
and Streamable HTTP transports, plus built-in auth providers (google-credentials, OAuth).
MCP support extends beyond tools topromptsandresources(the broader MCP spec), which
most other systems’ adapters omit. Slash commands (/agent,/mcp,/skill,/memory,/help) are
themselves extensible via skill metadata, allowing extensions to register new commands.
Mistral Vibe: Skills, MCP, Custom Tools, Agents, and Hooks.Mistral Vibe’s
extensibility surface has grown to five orthogonal axes. Skills follow theagentskills.iospec-
ification [43]: each is a directory containing aSKILL.mdwith YAML frontmatter, discovered
from.agents/skills/,.vibe/skills/,~/.vibe/skills/,~/.agents/skills/, and any user-
configured paths—now supplemented by built-in skills (including a “self-awareness” skill that

12.2 Protocol-Based Interfaces
Mini-SWE-Agentuses Python Protocols for structural subtyping: any class implementing the
Model,Agent, orEnvironmentprotocol can be substituted without inheritance. This is the lightest-
weight extensibility mechanism, requiring no registration, no manifest, and no SDK, just interface
conformance.
12.3 Configuration-Driven Extensibility
Aider’s model registry contains per-model metadata for 350+ models, including edit format, weak
model name, cache control settings, extra API parameters, reasoning tag handling, and editor model
configuration. This data-driven approach enables support for new models without code changes.
Codex’s layered TOML configuration now extendsabovethe user: mobile-device-management
(MDM), host-wide system, and enterprise cloud-delivered layers enter as base defaults beneath user
(config.tomlplus named profiles), project (.codexdirectory), and session-flag scopes, while a
separately composed requirements/constraint engine (fed from the same MDM, system, and cloud
sources) validates and can hard-restrict what the lower layers may set. It is the most enterprise-
governance-oriented configuration model in the corpus.Mistral Vibenow mirrors the layered
approach (six layers merged by aConfigBuilderwith per-field merge strategies), making explicit
config-layer stacks a two-system pattern.
OpenClaw’s config system is the most complex in the corpus: a Zod-validatedopenclaw.json
with hundreds of nested structures, plus a large auto-generated TypeScript file of validation rules
for all bundled channels alongside a hand-maintained schema-help surface.
12.4 Model Context Protocol (MCP)
MCP remains a cross-cutting extension mechanism, supported byClaude Code,Codex,Gem-

of 10 coding-first).OpenHandsimplements MCP viaMCPToolAction/MCPToolObservationevent

sse) and first-class authentication up to full OAuth flows with token state.Hermes’s support
is near-complete (stdio/Streamable HTTP/SSE, resources, prompts, sampling, elicitation, OAuth;
only prompt-list-change notifications ignored), andHermesisitselfan MCP server, matching
OpenClaw’s channel bridge.OpenCodecovers tools, prompts, resources, and resource templates
with OAuth dynamic client registration (sampling and elicitation explicitly disabled with tracking-
issue comments). The corpus’s one principled holdout isPi, whose documentation rejects MCP

13.2 The Twin Absences: No Agentic Frameworks, No Code RAG
Two technologies that the broader LLM-application literature treats as central to any production
agent turn out to be missing from all eleven systems.8 For this analysis, the absences are at least
as informative as the presences.
Absence 1: agentic frameworks.Every dependency manifest was inspected, and every source
tree grepped for imports of the widely deployed agentic frameworks: LangChain, LangGraph, Lla-
maIndex, AutoGen, CrewAI, Pydantic AI, Genkit, Haystack agents, Semantic Kernel, Google’s
ADK, and several smaller libraries (Smolagents, Swarm, Agno). Across roughly 4M lines of Python,
TypeScript, and Rust, no production agent code path imports any of them—a result worth savor-
ing in two directions:Gemini CLIuses neither of Google’s own frameworks (Genkit, ADK), and
Hermes’s only “LangChain” strings sit inside bundled skilldocumentationteaching the agent how

overaider’s own documentation—an opt-in feature dependency, not agent-loop orchestration. And

layer, not an agent-orchestration framework, but the first corpus system whose inner loop plumb-
ing is a third-party SDK at all (an in-house replacement client sits behind a flag, suggesting the
dependency is transitional).

Why the twin absences matter.The first absence corroborates advice from the agent designers
themselves. Schluntz & Zhang’sBuilding Effective Agents[16] cautions that frameworks “often
create extra layers of abstraction that can obscure the underlying prompts and responses, making
them harder to debug,” and recommends starting from raw SDK calls. That advice appeared in
December 2024; the source-code audit we run here, in 2026, shows that every commercial provider
has followed it. This is worth noting because the broader Python LLM-application community has
kept investing in framework abstractions over the same period. Production SWE agents appear to
operate on a different complexity budget from generic LLM applications. Hand-rolled debuggable
code wins out over reusable abstractions once the agent is mutating real source code, because the
failuremodes(silentpromptcorruption, opaquecaching, version-incompatibletoolschemas)become
too expensive to shrug off.

=== PAGE 56 ===
The second absence is consistent with recent guidance from Rajasekaran et al. [19] (Septem-
ber 2025), who favor “just-in-time” (JIT) retrieval while recommending a hybrid approach: “We
don’tmemorizeentirecorpusesofinformation, butratherintroduceexternalorganizationandindex-
ing systems like file systems, inboxes, and bookmarks to retrieve relevant information on demand.”
The blog post does not flatly reject pre-indexed retrieval, but its practical recommendations all point
toward JIT methods. Every system in the corpus follows this approach, even though repository-level
retrieval over code is a well-studied area: RepoCoder [60], RepoBench [61], and CrossCodeEval [62]
all build retrieval pipelines for code, and Long Code Arena [64] provides matching long-context eval-
uation. Wang et al.’s CodeRAG-Bench [63] asks the very question this section raises, “can retrieval
augment code generation?”, and finds gains that are highly variable across tasks: substantial for
documentation-lookup and library-use scenarios but marginal or absent for tasks where the model
already has sufficient parametric knowledge. The inconsistency of these gains may help explain why
productionSWEagentsskiptheRAGlayerentirelyratherthaninvestingintask-dependentretrieval
routing. The reasons are domain-specific. Code carries dense deterministic structural metadata—
file paths, language servers, tree-sitter parses, type information—that semantic-similarity retrieval
cannot replicate (and that structure-aware tools such as RepoAgent [65] andAider’sRepoMap
exploit directly). Code changes minute to minute, so pre-indexed embeddings are stale almost by
construction. Every coding environment already ships a near-optimal retrieval system in the form of
ripgrep,find, andglob. On the typical SWE-agent task, RAG adds operational cost (embedding
compute, vector-store maintenance, drift management) without offering marginal value.
Observation 9.Two technologies that the broader LLM-application literature treats as central
are missing from all eleven systems—a finding that survived a threefold corpus expansion and a
three-month re-audit. No system uses a general-purpose agentic framework in its agent runtime
(LangChain, LangGraph, AutoGen, CrewAI, or any of a dozen others checked;Gemini CLI
uses neither of Google’s own); every loop is hand-rolled in the host language’s async primitives,
withOpenCode’s use of a provider-abstraction SDK for inner plumbing as the closest boundary
case. No system uses vector-embedding RAG for code retrieval; all rely onripgrep, tree-sitter,
glob, and auto-discovered Markdown context files, and where conversation-scale recall is needed,
the production answer is lexical search (Hermes’s SQLite FTS5) or, in exactly one default
configuration (OpenClaw), hybrid embeddings over chat history—never over the source tree.
Production harnesses operate on a different complexity budget from generic LLM applications:
debuggability and prompt transparency outweigh framework reuse when the failure mode is
mutating real code. Section 14.2 gives the absence its historical resolution.
Observation 10.The architectural patterns described in Anthropic’sEffective Agentsengi-
neering series (December 2024 through September 2025) line up closely with the architectures
observed across the four provider-native systems, which were built independently. Whether this
reflects shared empirical reality, public-guidance influence, or both is an open question.

13.4 Trade-off Framework
We formalize five fundamental trade-off axes:
Axis 1: Simplicity vs. Capability.Mini-SWE-Agent’s minimal scaffold reportedly achieves
74%+ on SWE-Bench Verified, whileCodex’s far larger codebase reports 69.1%. These figures are
not directly comparable (different underlying models, different evaluation runs, different deployment
configurations:Mini-SWE-Agentis unsandboxed andCodexis sandboxed) and we do not draw
a head-to-head conclusion from them. What the gap does illustrate, qualitatively, is that the
additional code inCodexis not invested in raw task-completion logic: a substantial share goes into
safety (cross-platform sandboxing), user experience (TUI, streaming), extensibility (MCP, plugins),
and robustness. Whether the production scaffolding produces measurable improvements on the
same benchmark under matched conditions is an open question that this study does not answer
(see Section 15 on threats to validity).
Axis2: Safetyvs.Autonomy.Systemswithmoresafetyinfrastructure(Codex,Claude Code)
impose more friction on agent actions. Systems with less safety (Mini-SWE-Agent,Aider) enable
faster execution but are unsuitable for enterprise deployment without additional safeguards.
Axis 3: Provider Coupling vs. Agnosticism.Claude CodeandCodexexploit provider-
specific features (prompt caching, extended thinking, model-specific prompts) at the cost of vendor
lock-in. LiteLLM-based systems sacrifice these optimizations for provider flexibility.
Axis 4: Monolithic vs. Modular.Codex’s 126-crate, million-line Rust workspace optimizes
for type safety and performance.Mini-SWE-Agent’s single-file agent maximizes comprehensi-
bility.Pirelocates the entire axis into runtime composition: a minimal core plus an extension
event bus.OpenClaw’s plugin SDK andOpenCode’s client/server split enable extensibility and
embeddability respectively. Each point on this spectrum serves different user populations.
Axis 5: Scaffold Complexity vs. Model Capability.This is the most philosophically inter-
esting axis.Mini-SWE-Agent’s competitive benchmark performance demonstrates that current
models are capable enough to succeed with minimal scaffolding. Production systems invest in
scaffolding not because models need it for task completion, but because users need it for safety,
reliability, and workflow integration.

14 The Platform Turn
The preceding sections dissected the harness as an artifact. This section argues that the artifact has
changed category: between 2025 and mid-2026, the coding-agent harness completed a turn fromtool
toplatform—a runtime with its own extension ecosystem, package economics, governance layers,
switching costs, and, since June 2026, its own meta-layer. The April edition of this study advanced
a cautious version of this claim as the “CLI-as-framework hypothesis.” The evidence accumulated
since—in the corpus’s own source trees, in the vendor SDKs, and in the market—lets us state it as
a thesis.
14.1 From Hypothesis to Thesis: The CLI as Framework
The standard reading of the absence of LangChain, AutoGen, and the rest (Observation 9) is that
production harnesses do notneedframeworks. But there is a second reading: they do not need
frameworks because the harnessis itself the framework—the default orchestration layer through
which developers ship software, not merely a tool they query.
Consider what a framework traditionally provides: a loop, a tool registry, a memory layer, a
configuration surface, an extension mechanism. Every one of these is present in every produc-
tion system studied here.Claude Codeships a streaming ReAct loop, 43 registered tools, a
compaction-based memory manager, CLAUDE.md-driven configuration, MCP for integrations and
Skills for capability bundles—andCodex,Gemini CLI,Mistral Vibe,Hermes, andOpenCode
do the same, each in its own idiom (Pimatches every element but MCP, which it pointedly rejects
in favor of CLI tools plus its extension bus). The difference is that the “framework” is not a library
the developer imports into their code; it is a runtime the developer worksinside of. The developer’s
“program” is a natural-language task plus a tree of Markdown files (CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md,
GEMINI.md, SKILL.md) that configure the agent’s behavior, tool access, and domain knowledge.
The “API” is the filesystem, the terminal, and git.
Four convergent signals from the corpus support this reading.
Signal 1: Skills as declarative programs.Nine of the eleven systems implement Skills (Ob-
servation 8): directories containing aSKILL.mdwith instructions, optional scripts, and metadata.
A skill is, functionally, a program written for an LLM runtime rather than for a CPU runtime. It
declares what the agent should do (SKILL.mdbody), when it should activate (pathsfrontmatter in
Claude Code,PathTriggerrules inOpenHands,requiresinOpenClaw), and what tools it
may use. This is the structural analogue of a plugin in a traditional framework, except the program-
ming language is English plus YAML frontmatter—and the layer now has registries, supply-chain
security, and agent authors (Section 12.5).
Signal 2: Hooks and event buses as the extension substrate.In April, user-facing
lifecycle hooks were aClaude Codedistinctive. By July they are the dominant extension
substrate—nine of the eleven systems, with onlyAiderandMini-SWE-Agentabstaining:
Codexships hooks whose event names areverbatimClaude Code’s;OpenHandswires
pre_tool_use/post_tool_use/stophooks intoAgent.step(), with anagentallowed as a hook
handler;Mistral Vibeaddedhooks.tomlshell hooks (deny, rewrite inputs, append context)
alongside its internal middleware pipeline;Gemini CLIexposes eleven loop-lifecycle events to
extensions;Piisan event bus, with∼33 typed events spanning tool interception to raw provider
I/O;OpenCode’s plugins are functions returning twenty typed hooks. This is the same extension
model that web frameworks and build systems have used for decades, applied to an LLM execution
loop. The developer is not writingforthe agent; they are writinginsideit.
Signal 3: The disappearing boundary between tool and workflow.In a traditional de-
velopment workflow, the developer writes code, runs tests, reads logs, commits, and opens PRs as
discrete steps. In a harness workflow, these steps are delegated to the agent via natural-language
59
=== PAGE 60 ===
instructions and executed through the agent’s tool system. The agent loopisthe workflow engine:
Claude Code’s coordinator phases,Codex’supdate_plantool and/goalworkflows,Open-
Hands’s judge-audited goal loop. When the developer types a task and the agent spawns sub-
agents, dispatches tools, manages context, and produces the result, the CLI has subsumed the role
of the build system, the task runner, and the IDE.
Signal 4: The harness as a service surface.The clearest post-April signal: harnesses now
ship the interfaces of platforms.OpenCodeembeds an HTTP server publishing an OpenAPI
spec and a generated SDK, with every UI—TUI, desktop, web, IDE plugin, CI action—as a client.

teleports local sessions to a hosted runtime;Claude Codepairs its SDK with a hosted Managed
Agents API. A runtime with clients, SDKs, and hosted tiers is not a tool with an ecosystem; it is a
platform with distribution.

14.3 Platform Economics: Marketplaces, Switching Costs, Governance
Three developments from the ninety-day window are textbook platform economics.
Marketplaces.Codexgrew a plugin marketplace (GitHub/git/URL sources, in-conversation
install approval, workspace sharing); skills registries appeared in four systems with trust tiers,
provenance verification, and quarantine (Section 12.5);OpenClawexternalized its official plugins
to npm. Capability distribution now has app-store mechanics—including the app store’s security
pathologies, as SkillProbe’s audit of published skills shows [89].
Switching costs.Codexships a first-classimporter forClaude Code’s on-disk state: it de-
tects~/.claude/projectssession JSONL files, converts them intoCodexrollout items under
an import ledger, and offers to translate~/.claude/settings.jsoninto~/.codex/config.toml.
Vendors writing importers for each other’s session stores is the stage of platform competition where
user data becomes the moat—and the countervailing force is equally visible:OpenCodereads
Claude Code’s skills directory,OpenHandsreadsClaude Code’s plugin manifests and hosts
rival harnesses outright, and the meta-harness arbitrages every vendor’s lock-in mechanisms into its
adapter surface.
Enterprise governance.Codex’s configuration stack now extends above the user—MDM-
managed preferences, host-wide system files, and enterprise cloud-delivered bundles feed both base
defaults and a constraint engine that can hard-restrict what user and project scopes may set.
Google, meanwhile, announcedGemini CLI’s transition toward an Antigravity-branded CLI—
closed-source, whereGemini CLIis Apache-2.0 (Section 3.7)—and withdrew its consumer free tier,
and the SpaceX–xAI merger plus the announced $60B Cursor acquisition (pending as of July 2026)
priced the harness layer at acquisition scale. Governance layers, gated tiers, and acquisition-scale
pricing are the signatures of platforms, not of standalone tools.
14.4 The Meta-Harness Layer
Omnigent[25] is not a twelfth harness; it is a bet that the harness has become a commod-
ity component and that the durable value sits one layer up. Databricks open-sourced it in
June 2026 (Apache 2.0,∼1M lines total,∼312K of production Python): a four-tier process
topology (server⇄host daemon⇄runner⇄per-conversation harness subprocess) whose adapter
boundaryis, byexplicitdesign, arecursivesubsetofOmnigent’sownpublicRESTAPI.Itsregistry

15 Discussion
15.1 The Anthropic Effective-Agents Series as Empirical Validation
Between December 2024 and September 2025, Anthropic published four engineering articles offering
prescriptive guidance on agent design:Building Effective Agents[16],Effective Context Engineering
for AI Agents[19],Writing Effective Tools for AI Agents[18], and the case studyHow We Built Our
Multi-Agent Research System[17]. Across the four pieces, a coherent design philosophy emerges:
hand-rolled loops in preference to framework abstractions, a small set of high-signal tools in prefer-
ence to wrap-everything APIs, just-in-time structural retrieval in preference to pre-indexed RAG,
orchestrator-worker patterns in preference to flat monoliths, transparency about agent planning,
and explicit context budgeting as an engineering discipline.
Eleven independently developed systems (only one of which,Claude Code, is from Anthropic)
line up with these prescriptions:
•“Don’t use frameworks unless necessary” [16]: 0 of 11 systems use LangChain, LangGraph,
AutoGen, CrewAI, ADK, LlamaIndex, Pydantic AI, Genkit, or Semantic Kernel in the agent
runtime (Observation 9).
•“ACI matters as much as HCI” [16] (the concept originates in this article; Aizawa et al. [18]
extend it with practical tooling advice) and “few thoughtful tools” [18]:Aider’s 13 poly-
morphic edit formats,Claude Code’s deferred tool loading,Codex’s custom patch format,
Hermes’s nine-strategy edit chain, andOpenCode’s model-conditional tool surfaces are all
heavy ACI investments (Observation 4).
•“Just-in-time over RAG” [19]: 0 of 11 systems use vector embeddings over code; all usegrep,
tree-sitter, glob, and the file system, plus auto-discovered Markdown context files (Observa-
tion 9).

•“Orchestrator-workersforparallelizable, context-spanningtasks”[17]: emergesinClaude Code

There is one tension in the guidance that we should call out. Hadfield et al. [17] caution that

this study (eight countingOpenClaw’s protocol-layer variant) nevertheless implement coordinator-

mostly used for breadth-first exploration phases (parallel codebase research) rather than for parallel
implementation, which is consistent with Hadfield et al.’s caveat once the unit of analysis shifts from
the system as a whole to the individual workflow phase.
15.2 The Minimalism Argument
Mini-SWE-Agent’s competitive self-reported benchmark performance (74%+ on SWE-Bench Ver-
ified) with a minimal scaffold and a single bash tool undercuts the assumption that rich tool ecosys-
tems are necessary. The finding echoes the CodeAct [3] argument that executable code subsumes
discrete tool actions. For benchmark evaluation alone, most of the scaffolding in the production
systems turns out to be overhead.
The counter-argument is that benchmarks measure only a narrow slice of the value proposition.
Production systems (Claude Code,Codex,Gemini CLI,Mistral Vibe,OpenHands) invest
heavily in things that benchmarks do not reward:
•Safety: Preventing destructive actions on user codebases.
•User experience: Streaming responses, rich terminal UIs, progress tracking.
•Extensibility: Supporting custom tools, MCP servers, plugins.
•Robustness: Handling edge cases, stuck detection, error recovery.
•Cost management: Prompt caching, context compaction, model selection.
These concerns are invisible to SWE-Bench but critical for production adoption.

similar but lighter point on the spectrum: a cross-platform sandbox (reusing OS binaries) plus four
explicit approval modes (PLAN/DEFAULT/AUTO_EDIT/YOLO) and per-mode TOML policies.

15.4 The Model–Agent Co-design Thesis
The coupling between agent scaffolds and foundation models runs in both directions.
The model shapes the agent.Codex’s per-model prompts—now server-delivered catalog
data spanning GPT-5.2 through the 5.6 family, with per-model tool modes and multi-agent tool
generations—make explicit that scaffold design has to evolve with model capabilities, and that
the vendor intends to drive that evolution without client releases.Claude Code’s extended-
thinking integration (budget tokens, thinking blocks) exploits Claude-specific reasoning features.
Gemini CLI’sModelRouterServicetreats model selection itself as a runtime scheduling decision,
dispatching each request to the cheapest sufficient Gemini variant—and absorbing a full model-
generation shift (2.5→3.x defaults) within one quarter; the same idea is studied formally as Hybrid
LLM [84], RouteLLM [83], and the FrugalGPT cascade [82].Mistral Vibe’sreasoning_effort
mapping andThinkChunkparsing exploit Mistral’s reasoning enum.Aider’s model-aware edit

replacement) and its nine-prompt model-family matrix.OpenHandsships model-specific tool
presets(default, gemini, gpt5, planning)—co-design reaching even a multi-provider system’s tool
surface.
The agent shapes model usage.Claude Code’s deferred tool loading reduces prompt tokens,
changing the model’s effective context window.Claude Code’s prompt-caching boundary splits
the system prompt to maximize cache hits, structuring the prompt around cache economics as much
as around logical organization; Gim et al. [81] provide the systems-level grounding for the modular
attention-reuse strategy this implicitly assumes.Gemini CLIkeeps extended-thinking traces out
of persisted history at response-recording time, so they never leak into future cache lookups (and
strips thought signatures on auth switch).Mistral Vibe’s middleware pipeline lets per-turn token-
budget policies be composed orthogonally to the loop body.OpenHands’s condensers selectively
forget events, shaping what the model “remembers.”

tures in lockstep with model updates—Codexmost literally, via its server-delivered model cata-

15.5 The Scaffold–Capability Frontier (a Guiding Intuition)
We close with a guiding intuition rather than a formal hypothesis: across a fixed task distribution
and a fixed model, scaffold complexity and observed task success appear to trace a roughly concave
curve. A floor below which the agent cannot operate at all (no loop, no tool, no memory, no
result), a steep early-gain region where adding a small set of structural elements (a bash tool, a
read/write tool, a Markdown context file) raises success rapidly, and a diminishing-returns plateau
where further scaffold work pays off in operational concerns (safety, UX, extensibility) rather than
in completion rate. We label this picture thescaffold–capability frontierand treat it as a hypothesis
to be tested, not as a proven property of the design space.
Two independent results in the literature are consistent with this picture without confirming
it.Mini-SWE-Agent’s reported∼74%+ on SWE-Bench Verified with a deliberately minimal
scaffold suggests that, for SWE-Bench-style tasks on a frontier model, the floor is already well
below most production scaffolds. Lin et al. [85] report that theirAgentic Harness Engineering
(AHE) system, starting from a bash-only seed comparable toMini-SWE-Agentand automatically
evolving the harness using observability-driven feedback, reaches 71.9% on SWE-Bench Verified.
Their component-level ablation finds thatstructuralharness elements carry the improvement (tools
+3.3pp, middleware+2.2pp, long-term memory+5.6pp), while the system prompt aloneregresses
performance (−2.3pp); the evolved harness also transfers across four model families. We read this
as suggestive evidence that structural scaffold is more portable than prose-level prompt strategy,
consistent with our model–agent co-design discussion at the architectural (rather than weights)
level.
What an operational definition would require is at minimum: a fixed task distributionT, a
fixed modelM, a metric for scaffold complexity (LoC, tool count, structural feature count, or some
weighted combination), and a target success ratep. A “minimum viable scaffold for(T, M, p)” would
then be the lower envelope of scaffold-complexity values whose empirical success rate onTwithM
exceedsp. We do not produce such a measurement here. Doing so would require a controlled
cross-system run study that this paper deliberately avoids (see Threats to Validity, Section 15). We
flag the scaffold–capability frontier as a question for follow-on empirical work, and note that the
relevant minimum almost certainly varies across:
•Task complexity: Multi-file refactoring may require more sophisticated tool support than
single-file bug fixes.
•Safety requirements: Enterprise deployments demand scaffolding that benchmarks don’t
measure.
•Interaction patterns: Long-running interactive sessions benefit from memory management
that short benchmark runs don’t need.
15.6 Threats to Validity
A study of this shape has real limits, and it is worth being explicit about them.
The analysis rests on source-code reading, not runtime measurement. We do not claim to know
how fast any of these systemsare; we claim to know how they arestructured. Where the paper
reports benchmark numbers, they come from the systems’ own documentation, and the last decimal
place is not the interesting part.
The qualitative scoring underlying the architectural comparisons involves judgment calls. We
tried to anchor each score in a specific implementation detail, and we welcome disagreement from

=== PAGE 67 ===
anyone who reads the same codebases and scores them differently. We deliberately avoid line-
number citations, and we date all code-size figures to the July-2026 pins: every system in the
corpus is actively developed, and finer precision would be obsolete by the time the paper is read.
All versions are pinned to exact release tags and commits (Table 3), the April-2026 snapshots
are retained for the longitudinal comparison of Section 14.5, and—a lesson that quarter taught us
directly—we now distinguishinventoryclaims, which we date, fromstructuralclaims, which we
expect to endure. Three observations of the April edition were substantively revised in this one on
new evidence; the revisions are integrated in place and flagged where they occur.
TheClaude Codeanalysis is the weakest link on reproducibility. It draws on a publicly
circulated source snapshot from March 2026 rather than an official release, and the shipping binary
has evolved considerably since (we track its changelog-level evolution but do not treat changelog
claims as source evidence). The architectural claims should still be testable against the public
claude-agent-sdkand the behavior of the shipping binary, but we acknowledge the asymmetry
with the other ten systems, whose source trees are trivially re-derivable from public Git history.
The framework-absence finding (Observation 9) is strong but structurally conservative. We
checked dependency manifests and performed import grep across three languages—twice, three
months apart, across twelve trees including the meta-harness; we did not trace internal forks,
plugins loaded via dynamicimportlib/require, or transpiled distributions. A company could in
principle use LangChain inside a production SWE agent through a code path we did not inspect.
We would be surprised, but not shocked.
The Anthropic-guidance-to-observation mapping (Observation 10) is suggestive but does not by
itself establish causation. The right follow-up is interview work with engineers at each vendor; we
did not do that.
We also compared each system independently rather than running all eleven on a shared task
set. A head-to-head execution study would turn some of the qualitative scoring into measurable
quantities. It would also cost an order of magnitude more effort than this paper, and we chose the
tractable scope.

ACP). Six of eleven corpus systems ship it. Foragent↔agentmesh topologies,A2Aremains
Gemini CLI’s bet alone—defensible, unproven. Formain-agent↔sub-agentcoordination
within your own runtime, donotadopt either (see Recommendation 12): in-process primitives
are what production systems use, and even the cross-process exceptions (Pi’s JSONL extension,
Hermes’s SQLite-blackboard swarm) avoid the standard protocols for that role.
16.8 Extensibility
Recommendation 14.UseSkillsfor capability templates (workflows, domain knowledge,
procedural recipes) andMCPfor external integrations (Slack, databases, internal APIs)—in
that order of priority.Evidence: Observation 8: skills now lead MCP in adoption (9/11 vs. 8/11),
discovery is cross-vendor (OpenCodereads~/.claude/skills), and distribution has registries
and provenance. MCP remains the right layer for running external processes;Pidemonstrates
the defensible minimal position (skills plus CLI tools with READMEs, no MCP). If you install
third-party skills, treat them as packages: trust tiers, scanning, and quarantine (Hermes) are
the emerging baseline [89].
16.9 What Not to Build (Anti-Patterns)
Recommendation 15. Do notuse LangChain, LangGraph, AutoGen, CrewAI, LlamaIndex,
Pydantic AI, Genkit, Google ADK, or Semantic Kernel for the agent runtime.Evidence: Ob-
servation 9; 0/11 production harnesses in the corpus use these frameworks—Gemini CLIskips
Google’s own. Schluntz & Zhang [16] explicitly warn: “frameworks often create extra layers of
abstraction that can obscure the underlying prompts and responses, making them harder to de-
bug.” Use raw SDK calls—or, the 2026 corollary (Section 14.2): if you want a batteries-included
starting point, the harness SDKs (Claude Agent SDK,openai-codex,openhands-sdk)arethe
framework layer now, with the corpus’s patterns built in.
Recommendation 16. Do notbuild a vector-embedding retrieval layer for code.Evidence:
Observation 9; 0/11 systems do this. The embeddings that do appear serveconversation memory
(OpenClaw’s default hybrid search;Hermes’s opt-in plugins), andHermesshows lexical
SQLite FTS5 sufficing for that role at 642K-line scale. If you believe your domain needs semantic
code retrieval, first prove it improves overripgrep+tree-sitteron a held-out task set before
adding the infrastructure.
Recommendation 17. Do notwrap every upstream SaaS API as a 1-to-1 tool.Evidence:
Aizawa et al. [18]; “a common error we’ve observed is tools that merely wrap existing software
functionality or API endpoints.” Consolidate instead.
Recommendation 18. Do notover-engineer stuck detection—but do ship the cheap caps,
which cost a dozen lines.Evidence:Claude CodeandCodexstill ship no automated stuck
detection and deliver production-quality agents. The floor moved, though: evenMini-SWE-
Agentnow caps consecutive malformed responses and wall-clock time,OpenCode’s doom-
loop check is a three-identical-calls counter routed to a permission ask, andHermeshashes
call signatures but ships the hard stopdisabled, trusting warnings.OpenHands’s five-scenario
StuckDetectorandGemini CLI’s hybrid hash+LLM service remain platform-tier investments.

17 Conclusion and Future Work
This paper has laid out a source-code-level anatomy of eleven LLM-powered coding harnesses—
one per major commercial LLM provider (Claude Code/Anthropic,Codex/OpenAI,Gem-
ini CLI/Google,Mistral Vibe/Mistral) plus seven open-source designs—together with a meta-
harness contrast point, a longitudinal diff of one quarter’s evolution, and an account of the field’s
turn into platforms.
17.1 Key Takeaways
1.Architecture matters, but not in the way the current discourse implies.The sophis-
tication of the agent loop does not predict benchmark performance (Mini-SWE-Agent’s 50-
line loop reports results in the frontier range). It does, however, predict production readiness:
safety infrastructure, user experience, extensibility—and increasingly clients and transport,
where the largest systems now carry most of their mass.
2.The model–agent relationship is co-evolutionary, and coupling is about the update
loop, not capability.Provider-native optimizations (cache boundaries, thinking budgets,
model-specific prompts, routing) are exercised frombothends of the coupling spectrum: the
72
=== PAGE 73 ===
vendor systems natively, andHermes,Pi, andOpenCodefrom multi-provider substrates by
paying the per-provider conditioning cost centrally. What only vendors retain is server-side
co-evolution—Codexre-tunes prompts, reasoning tiers, and even its multi-agent tooling per
model release from a catalog endpoint, no client update required.
3.Safety is architecturally expensive—and a choice, not a consequence of scale.
Codex’s cross-platform sandboxing (now with vendored bubblewrap) remains a substantial
investment benchmarks never measure, and the meta-harness re-pays the same bill one layer
up. But the April size-implies-sandbox correlation broke:HermesandOpenCodeare among
the largest systems with zero OS-level isolation, spending instead on content-borne threat
defense and syntax-aware permissioning.
4.Multi-agent orchestration converged on hierarchical patterns, and the protocols
found a third role.Coordinator-worker shapes now appear in seven systems. ACP serves
not only the editor↔agent boundary (six corpus adopters) but a role outside its design
brief:harness hosting, withOpenHandsrunningClaude Code,Codex, orGemini CLI
as interchangeable backends. A2A remainsGemini CLI’s cross-vendor mesh bet. Sub-agent
coordination stays in-process in eight of the nine multi-agent systems.
5.The extensibility standards resolved: skills lead.Skills (9/11 systems) overtook MCP
(8/11) whenPibroke the tie—implementing agentskills.io while rejecting MCP outright.
The skills layer acquired registries, trust tiers, provenance verification, cross-vendor discovery
(OpenCodereads~/.claude/skills), and its first agent authors (Hermes’s self-improving
loop,Gemini CLI’s extraction inbox).
6.Thetwinabsencesarereal, re-verified, andnowhistoricallyexplained.Acrosseleven
systems and roughly four million lines, no agent runtime uses a general-purpose agentic frame-
work, and none uses RAG over code—all rely on hand-rolled async loops and deterministic
structural retrieval. The absence held through a threefold corpus expansion and a three-
month re-audit, corroborating Anthropic’s published guidance [16, 19] and the independent
AHE result [85]. Its resolution is the harness–framework merger of Section 14.2.
7.TheAnthropiceffective-agentsseriesanticipatesthearchitecturesweobserve.The
four engineering articles published between December 2024 and September 2025 map closely
onto the patterns documented across eleven independently developed systems. Whether this
reflects shared empirical reality, guidance-shaped design, or both is a question worth pursuing
on its own.
8.The analysis is directly actionable.Section 16 distills 18 concrete, evidence-anchored
design recommendations for practitioners building new harnesses, together with a 90-line
minimum-viable-harness scaffold (Listing 3) that implements ten of them directly. Combined
with the source-code audit, this is, as far as we know, the first evidence-based how-to for the
discipline that is not a single-vendor whitepaper.
9.Theplatformturnisnolongerahypothesis.WhattheAprileditionadvancedcautiously
as the CLI-as-framework hypothesis, Section 14 now documents as an accomplished turn: the
harnesses ship as importable SDKs while the framework vendors ship harnesses (the merger
of Section 14.2); capability distribution has marketplaces, registries, trust tiers [89], and
agent authors; vendors write importers for each other’s on-disk state and expose MDM-grade
(mobile-device-management) governance; the agent itself is addressable as a model behind an
OpenAI-compatible endpoint; and a meta-harness orchestrates eleven vendor harnesses—five
of the systems studied here among them—behind one API (Section 14.4). The surrounding
ecosystem data points the same way: MCP’s 8000+ server ecosystem with app-store curation
pathologies [86], skills formalized as composable packages [87] with npm-like package man-
agers [88], 22–29% of GitHub projects bearing agent traces [91], and the “SE 3.0” framing of
development as orchestration [92]. The developer no longer programswiththe framework but

ating systems and IDEs have always produced [90]—with the meta-layer already arbitraging
it. Whether harnesses consolidate into a few dominant platforms, fragment into interoper-
able runtimes, or commoditize from above is the competitive question of the next year; the
architectural preconditions for all three outcomes are in the source code today.
17.2 Future Work
Several directions emerge from our analysis:
•Unified evaluation frameworksthat assess safety, user experience, cost efficiency, and
extensibility alongside correctness, addressing the gap between benchmark performance and
production readiness.
•Reference architecture specificationthat formalizes the patterns we identified (event
sourcing, policy-as-code, context forking, deferred tool loading, middleware pipelines, hash-
based loop detection, JIT repo context) into a reusable architectural framework.
•Formal verification of safety policiesfor policy-as-code systems, establishing guarantees
about what agent actions are permitted under given policy configurations.
•Empiricalstudyofmodel–agentco-evolution, trackinghowagentscaffoldschangeacross
model generations and quantifying the performance impact of model-specific optimizations.
Lin et al.’s AHE system [85] demonstrates that automatic harness evolution is already feasible;
longitudinal studies could now track how automatically evolved scaffolds compare to hand-
crafted ones across model generations.
•Cross-system benchmark suitethat runs identical tasks across all eleven systems under
controlled conditions, enabling direct comparison of the architectural trade-offs we identified.
•Inter-agent protocol adoption study, tracking ACP’s three roles (editor integration, har-
ness hosting, and—via A2A—the cross-vendor mesh) and measuring the overhead of hosted-
harness topologies such asOpenHands’s ACP backends andOmnigent’s adapter fleet.
•Longitudinal continuation: the April/July snapshot pair turns this study into a time
series; quarterly re-pinning would measure pattern-diffusion half-lives, prompt thinning, and
the platformization trajectory quantitatively rather than anecdotally.
•Meta-harness economics: whether commoditization-from-above (Section 14.4) captures
value durably, and whether the conformance-bench approach to harness capabilities becomes
a standard interface.
•Causation analysis of the Anthropic–industry alignmentvia interviews, design-doc
archaeology, and commit-history correlation, distinguishing public-guidance influence from
convergent engineering.
