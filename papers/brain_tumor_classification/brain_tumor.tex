\begin{abstract}
Accurate classification of brain tumors from MRI is a key step toward faster, more consistent diagnosis, but a lot of the reported accuracy numbers in this area come from a single train/test split, and single splits can be misleading -- a lucky or unlucky partition can make a model look better or worse than it really is. In this paper, we build a hybrid classification pipeline around a pretrained EfficientNetB0 backbone, fine-tuned in two stages, with class-weighted training to handle label imbalance. On top of the trained network, we try two different inference strategies: test-time augmentation (TTA) on the CNN's own softmax output, and a soft-voting ensemble of Random Forest, XGBoost, and SVM classifiers trained on the CNN's learned embeddings. Both are evaluated under 5-fold stratified cross-validation on a four-class, 7,023-image brain tumor MRI dataset (glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and no-tumor), rather than a single held-out split. CNN+TTA reaches 97.74\% $\pm$ 0.47\% accuracy on average across folds, and the embedding ensemble reaches 97.36\% $\pm$ 0.22\%, with noticeably less fold-to-fold variance. Per-class precision, recall, and F1 stay above 0.96 for all four classes. Because these numbers come from cross-validation rather than one lucky split, we believe they give a more honest picture of how well the pipeline would generalize, and support its use as a component in AI-assisted brain tumor screening.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A brain tumor develops when abnormal cells grow unchecked inside the brain \cite{r16}, and depending on where it forms, it can affect anything from motor function to memory to basic cognition. The current WHO classification recognizes dozens of distinct tumor types and grades \cite{louis2021who}, but for imaging-based triage the coarser split we use here -- glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, or no tumor at all -- is still the practical starting point most automated systems are built around. Because of this, clinicians place a lot of emphasis on catching tumors early and getting the diagnosis right the first time, since that is what determines whether treatment can even be planned properly \cite{r16}. The scale of the problem is not small either -- in the United States alone, the American Cancer Society estimates around 24,820 new malignant brain and CNS tumor diagnoses for 2025, with roughly 18,330 deaths \cite{r4}.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the imaging method of choice here, mainly because it shows soft tissue detail well and does not require anything invasive \cite{r16}. The catch is that after the scan is taken, a radiologist still has to look at it and make a judgment call, and that step is slow and not always consistent from one reader to another. This is exactly the gap automated tools are trying to close -- not necessarily to replace the radiologist, but to make the read faster and more repeatable \cite{r19}.

Deep learning, and CNNs in particular, have made a real dent in this problem over the last several years \cite{r16, r17}. That said, medical imaging datasets tend to be small compared to what these models usually want, so overfitting is a constant concern \cite{r19}. One common workaround is to combine a CNN with a more traditional classifier -- use the network for feature extraction and let something like a Random Forest handle the final decision \cite{r18, r20}. Data augmentation is the other standard trick for squeezing more out of a limited dataset \cite{r20}.

What is less talked about, though, is how these methods actually get evaluated. A lot of papers in this space report one number from one train/test split, and that number can shift quite a bit depending on which images happened to land in the test set. It is not really evidence that a model generalizes -- it is one sample of how it might perform. Cross-validation, where several different splits are tried and the results averaged, gives a much more honest picture, and it is what we lean on throughout this paper.

Concretely, we build a hybrid pipeline: a pretrained EfficientNetB0 backbone, fine-tuned in two stages, with class weighting to handle the imbalance across tumor types. On top of that, we compare two ways of turning the trained network's output into a final prediction -- test-time augmentation, and a soft-voting ensemble of classical classifiers (Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM) trained on the network's embeddings. Everything is evaluated with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on a four-class, 7,023-image brain tumor MRI dataset, and we report mean/standard-deviation accuracy rather than a single number, along with a look at where each class tends to get confused and how the two inference strategies compare.


\section{Related Work}

CNNs have been the workhorse of brain tumor classification for a while now, largely because they can pull out hierarchical features from an MRI slice without much manual feature engineering. Dorfner et al. \cite{r16} were among the early groups to show this worked well for both segmentation and classification, and part of their motivation was cutting down on the subjectivity that comes with manual reads. Kaifi \cite{r17} pushed this further with a modified softsign activation function, reporting 97.6\% accuracy on the same four-class glioma/meningioma/pituitary/no-tumor problem we tackle here. Both lines of work made it clear CNNs were useful here, but they also ran into the same two walls most groups do: limited interpretability, and a constant hunger for more labeled data than these datasets typically provide.

A natural response to the labeled-data problem has been to combine CNNs with more traditional classifiers rather than relying on the network end-to-end. Al-Zoghby et al. \cite{r18} did exactly this with a dual-CNN classifier published in \emph{Diagnostics}, reporting 97.3\% accuracy. Around the same time, Vision Transformers \cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} started showing up as an alternative worth considering, mainly because of how they handle long-range spatial relationships that convolutions can miss. Karagoz et al. \cite{r19} combined a Residual Vision Transformer with self-supervised pretraining specifically to deal with scarce labels, which is a reasonable angle given how small most public brain tumor datasets are. More recently, G\'omez-Guzm\'an et al. \cite{gomezguzman2025swin} benchmarked several pretrained CNNs alongside transformer backbones on the very same 7,023-image dataset we use here, with a Swin-Tiny transformer reaching 99.24\% on a single 75/25 split -- a useful reminder that transformer backbones are increasingly competitive with CNNs on this exact problem, even if, like nearly everything else in this table, the number comes from one split rather than a cross-validated estimate.

Ensemble methods and augmentation have also had their moment. Talukder et al. \cite{r20} combined contrast-enhancement preprocessing with an Inception V3-based transfer learning ensemble and reported 98.89\% accuracy across glioma, meningioma, and pituitary tumor. On the augmentation side, the usual toolbox -- rotation, flipping, and increasingly GAN-based synthesis \cite{goodfellow2014gan} -- keeps showing up as a way to stretch small datasets further, and self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled scans has been gaining traction for the same reason, particularly given how well transfer learning from ImageNet-scale pretraining tends to carry over to medical imaging despite the domain gap \cite{morid2021transfer}.

Two gaps stand out across most of this work, though. First, interpretability is still mostly an afterthought -- Grad-CAM and similar attention-based explanations get mentioned as future work far more often than they get actually reported. Second, and this is the one we care about most here, a large share of these papers report a single train/test split number rather than anything cross-validated, which makes it hard to know how much of the reported accuracy is real signal versus split luck -- a broader methodological failure mode that has been flagged across medical imaging ML generally, not just in this subfield \cite{varoquaux2022leakage}. Looking further out, combining MRI with other modalities like PET or CT, and moving toward lighter models that could realistically run intraoperatively, both seem like reasonable next steps for the field.


\section{Dataset}

We use a four-class brain tumor MRI dataset containing 7,023 T1-weighted images: 1,800 glioma, 1,800 meningioma, 1,800 pituitary tumor, and 1,623 no-tumor. It ships as separate Training and Testing folders, but we merge the two and re-split the whole thing ourselves using stratified 5-fold cross-validation -- partly because we wanted every image to get a turn as test data, and partly because the original split alone would only give us one accuracy number instead of the mean/std we were after.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Each image is decoded, resized to 224$\times$224, and normalized the way EfficientNet expects its input. Since the four classes are not perfectly balanced (no-tumor has noticeably fewer images than the other three), we use class-weighted training rather than oversampling -- a standard response to the class-imbalance problem in CNNs \cite{buda2018imbalance} -- weights are just the inverse class frequency within each training fold, computed with \texttt{sklearn}'s \texttt{compute\_class\_weight} in \texttt{balanced} mode, and fed straight into the loss.

For augmentation we use a fairly standard set -- random rotation, translation, zoom, horizontal flip, and a bit of contrast jitter -- wrapped in a Keras \texttt{Sequential} block. It does double duty here: during training it's the usual overfitting guard, and at inference we reuse the same block for test-time augmentation (Section~\ref{sec:inference}).

\subsection{CNN Backbone and Two-Stage Fine-Tuning}
We use EfficientNetB0 \cite{tan2019efficientnet} pretrained on ImageNet as the backbone -- it was an easy pick given how well its compound scaling of depth, width, and resolution tends to trade off accuracy against parameter count on exactly this kind of image-classification task. On top of its output we add global average pooling, batch normalization \cite{ioffe2015batchnorm}, dropout (0.4) \cite{srivastava2014dropout}, a 128-unit dense layer that doubles as our embedding (batch-normalized, dropout 0.3 again), and finally a softmax over the four classes.

Training happens in two stages, which turned out to matter more than we initially expected. Stage 1 freezes the backbone completely and trains only the new head (Adam \cite{kingma2015adam}, $10^{-3}$, 8 epochs) -- the point is just to let the randomly initialized head settle down before we risk touching the pretrained weights. Stage 2 then unfreezes the top 80\% of the backbone and fine-tunes the whole thing together at a much lower rate ($10^{-5}$, up to 35 epochs), with early stopping on validation loss (patience 10) and a reduce-on-plateau schedule (factor 0.5, patience 4). Unfreezing does cause a visible, temporary dip in accuracy right at the start of Stage 2 -- we saw this consistently across runs -- but it recovers within a few epochs and ends up well above where Stage 1 alone plateaus.

\subsection{Dual Inference Strategy}
\label{sec:inference}
Once the CNN is trained, there are at least two reasonable ways to turn it into a final prediction, and we did not want to just pick one arbitrarily, so we tried both:

\textbf{Test-time augmentation (TTA).} At test time we run each image through the model once clean and five more times with the same augmentations used in training, then average the softmax outputs, an approach with roots in the uncertainty-estimation literature \cite{wang2019tta}. It costs nothing extra in terms of training, just a bit more inference time.

\textbf{CNN-embedding ensemble.} Here we pull the 128-dimensional embedding out of the CNN's penultimate layer (i.e., right before the softmax) for every image, and train three classical models on top of it -- a Random Forest \cite{breiman2001randomforests} (300 trees, balanced class weights), XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost} (300 estimators, depth 5, learning rate 0.05), and an RBF SVM (balanced weights, probability outputs on) -- combined by soft voting. The idea is that the deep network already does the hard work of turning pixels into a useful representation, and a small ensemble on top might squeeze out a bit more robustness than the network's own softmax head.

\subsection{Cross-Validation Protocol}
We merge the Training and Testing folders and split the combined set into 5 stratified folds, so class proportions stay consistent everywhere. Inside each fold's training portion we carve out a further 85/15 stratified split for validation/early-stopping. Every single image ends up in the test set exactly once across the five folds, and what we report is the mean and standard deviation over those five runs, not any one of them in isolation. All training ran on Kaggle (P100 GPU, 25\,GB RAM), batch size 16.

\subsection{Interpretability: Grad-CAM Visualization}
\label{sec:gradcam}
High accuracy alone does not tell us whether the model is actually looking at the tumor, or just latching onto some incidental artifact in the images. To get at least a qualitative answer to that, we ran Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} on the trained network's last convolutional feature map. Fig.~\ref{fig:gradcam} shows one correctly classified example per class, heatmap overlaid on the original slice.

\caption{Grad-CAM visualizations (original, heatmap, overlay) for one correctly classified example per class. The glioma heatmap lines up almost exactly with the visibly abnormal, brighter tissue in the upper part of the slice. The meningioma one is even cleaner -- it locks onto a well-defined mass sitting right against the skull, which matches how meningiomas typically present (they grow attached to the dura, on the outside of the brain tissue proper). Pituitary tumor was the one we were least sure about going in, since the gland itself is tiny relative to the whole slice, but the heatmap still concentrates right on the sellar region at the skull base -- the correct anatomical spot. For no-tumor, there is no single hot spot at all; activation spreads out fairly symmetrically across both hemispheres, which is basically what you'd expect when there is nothing focal for the model to key on. Taken together, these four examples are reassuring -- the network does seem to be keying off real anatomy rather than some shortcut in the images -- though we should be upfront that Grad-CAM's resolution is coarse (it comes from a heavily downsampled feature map), so it cannot tell us much about precise tumor boundaries, and we have not tried to quantify the overlap between heatmap and lesion in any systematic way here.


\section{Discussion}
So which of the two inference strategies should you actually use? CNN+TTA wins on raw mean accuracy every time, but the embedding ensemble is markedly steadier across folds ($\pm$0.22\% vs.\ $\pm$0.47\%). Given that both come essentially for free once the CNN is already trained, our take is that this isn't really an either/or choice -- TTA if you want the best expected accuracy, the ensemble if you'd rather have less variance in what you get. Which one matters more probably depends on the deployment setting more than anything we can determine from this dataset alone.

The confusion patterns line up with what you'd expect clinically. Meningioma varies a lot in how it looks on imaging and its boundaries are not always crisp, so it makes sense that it was the class the model mixed up most often, mainly with glioma and pituitary tumor. No-tumor, having no lesion to confuse with anything, was reliably the easiest across every fold.


\section{Limitations}
A few things temper how far these results should be trusted. The dataset doesn't include a patient ID field, so our cross-validation is done at the image level -- we cannot rule out near-duplicate images landing on both sides of a fold, especially since the dataset itself is a merge of several public sources. This is precisely the kind of subject-level leakage that has been flagged as a recurring, often silent, source of inflated performance in medical imaging ML more broadly \cite{varoquaux2022leakage}, so we would rather name it explicitly than let it pass unmentioned. It's also worth remembering this is 2D slice classification, not full 3D volume analysis, so it's a simplified version of what a radiologist actually does. And since all the data comes from a fairly narrow set of source institutions and scanners, we genuinely don't know yet how well this would hold up on MRI from a different hospital or a different machine. These are the gaps the future work below tries to address.


\section{Conclusion}
We set out to build a hybrid brain tumor classifier -- pretrained EfficientNetB0 backbone, class-weighted training, and two ways of turning that into a final prediction (TTA, and a Random Forest/XGBoost/SVM ensemble on the network's embeddings) -- and, just as importantly, to evaluate it properly with 5-fold stratified cross-validation instead of a single split. On a four-class, 7,023-image dataset, CNN+TTA averaged 97.74\% $\pm$ 0.47\% and the embedding ensemble 97.36\% $\pm$ 0.22\%, with every class holding precision and recall at 0.94 or above. Because these numbers come from cross-validation rather than one lucky test set, we think they're a fairer estimate of how this pipeline would actually perform, and a reasonable step toward a genuinely useful AI-assisted screening tool.


\section{Future Work}
A few directions seem worth chasing next. Multi-scale feature fusion or added attention in the backbone might help specifically with the boundary-ambiguous cases like meningioma that gave us the most trouble. Swapping in a CNN--Transformer hybrid or one of the newer state-space (Mamba-style) architectures as the backbone is another angle we didn't have time to explore here. On the interpretability side, Section~\ref{sec:gradcam}'s Grad-CAM results are encouraging but only qualitative -- a proper quantitative check, e.g. measuring heatmap-to-lesion overlap against annotated masks from a dataset like figshare, would carry a lot more weight than eyeballing four examples. It would also be worth tracking down or annotating patient identifiers for this dataset (or just using one that already has them) so that cross-validation can be done at the patient level instead of the image level, closing the gap we flagged above. And finally, none of this has been tested on MRI from outside this dataset's source institutions, so external validation is really the next necessary step before anyone should treat this as clinically ready.
